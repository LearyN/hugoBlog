I just turned 31. In my country, people start whispering about "mid-life crisis" after 35. So the clock is ticking, which means it's a good time to look back before I forget the details. Let me dump my brain onto the keyboard while the memories are still fresh.

## Chapter 0: Mountains and Bicycles (2014–2018)

At 18, I enrolled in a university nestled in the mountains to study Business Japanese. Four years passed, and somewhere along the way I picked up N1 — the highest level of Japanese proficiency — without really breaking a sweat.

But here's the thing: most of my time went into road cycling. I never became a great rider, but I did manage to stay reasonably healthy. Trade a few trophies for a body that doesn't fall apart? Worth it.

## Chapter 1: The Software Sales Company (2018–2021)

After graduation, I sent out exactly **two** résumés and went to **one** interview. That landed me my first job at a software company responsible for Japanese-market content marketing.

This company had no real in-house R&D, but boy, could they market. My job? Churning out Japanese blog content — high intensity, low output. If you visit `site:fonepaw.com` and search for 橋本学, you can still find my old work rotting there.

Here's the thing about the start: my salary was insultingly tiny. On top of that, I had crippling allergies, so I could only afford the cheapest antihistamines. The symptoms *plus* the side effects nearly broke me — there were days I couldn't function at all.

At my poorest, I was borrowing next month's paycheck through a fintech lending app, surviving an entire month on the cheapest egg noodles. That one left a scar.

But I grinded. Three years in, I went from junior content editor to **product owner**, running a Mac cleaner product for the Japanese market. The commissions got me a genuinely decent paycheck. And when COVID-19 hit, remote work kept my income steady — that pulled me through the worst of it.

Then leadership promoted me to head of link building. **Zzz.** It was dead boring. I lasted six months before walking away. Looking back, I wasn't manager material then — I'd rather do the work myself and keep learning.

## Chapter 2: The E-commerce Leviathan (2021)

Half a year of gap. In a 40-degree rental flat, I was too scared to turn on the AC. Electricity is expensive, okay?

The market was telling everyone "industrial manufacturing is the way," so I joined an e-commerce company. It was *huge*. Bloated. A thousand employees. And the Japan division? Ten people.

They ran this brutal "big-small week" schedule — work every other Saturday. My only real takeaway was dabbling in Shopify, back when DTC (direct-to-consumer) was all the rage and everyone wanted to escape Amazon.

Then it went sideways: my direct manager turned out to be a workplace-bullying psycho. I got out before my mental health got wrecked. Worst career decision I've made — but also a fast lesson in spotting red flags.

## Chapter 3: The Car Media Startup (2021–2023)

Next stop: an auto-media startup targeting Southeast Asia, incubated inside a bigger company. They were ambitious and already had real traction.

I came in as a junior SEO. Because their goals were big enough, they let me run my own SEO experiments. I absorbed knowledge like a sponge. This is where I leveled up from "guy who does SEO" to **an SEO expert who can hold the whole thing together**.

Two raises later, the big parent sold us off to a regional conglomerate — where we were deemed "surplus assets." Everyone got kicked out like stray dogs.

I was young and furious, so I fired up a lawsuit. That was a harsh lesson from society: even with zero legal compliance on their end (they didn't even show up to court), I lost. Twice. And to this day there's a huge chunk of money sitting in the court as collateral that I still can't get back. My faith in the judicial system took a direct hit.

## Chapter 4: The Second Car Media (Dec 2023 – Jul 2024)

A local internet company was assembling a Southeast Asia car-media team to ride the wave of Chinese car exports. I joined, got a solid offer, and built them a genuinely good website.

But I quickly realized the person in charge was — another jerk. I did my due diligence, shipped what I promised, and bounced.

## Chapter 5: The Small Company with a Giant Site (Jul 2024 – 2026)

Then I landed at a small company with an enormous website. Now *this* was fun. Challenges I'd never touched came at me fast: large-scale SEO, UGC, compliance, cybersecurity, traffic monetization, paid ads.

In two years, I basically became a different person.

But right as I leveled up, the company hit the ceiling of its growth curve. Leadership started panicking. And meanwhile, a bigger company wanted to take its car-media site overseas. So it was natural for me to leave.

## Chapter 6: Now

I keep watching good companies decline, and I keep saying goodbye to brilliant colleagues. I get it now — that's just the economic cycle. No organization or career lasts forever.

I just turned 31. In my country, people cross 35 and the "mid-life crisis" kicks in. My runway is getting shorter.

So — let's see what interesting things are waiting for me next. 🚴♂️
